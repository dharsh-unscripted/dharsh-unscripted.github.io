Politics - John Locke Competition

Published September 12, 2025

Edmund Burke celebrated the wisdom of "unlettered men". In a democracy, do the votes of the unlettered tend to protect a country against the bad ideas of the lettered, or do the votes of the lettered tend to protect a country against the bad ideas of the unlettered? The “Vote Leave Campaign” during Brexit highlighted a significant disparity between the elite and common voters. Remain voters have often criticized this election result, pointing to the prevalence of heuristics, low economic literacy, and politicization that has led to an irrational choice. [1] And in some ways, the numbers have borne out these fears: Brexit has cost the U.K. 30% of its trading partners, with small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) often suffering the most, as they now face extra paperwork, customs checks, and tax requirements. Some smaller firms have simply given up exporting to Europe altogether, while others have suffered increased logistical costs. [2] Critics highlight how campaigns focused more on slogans than on nuanced debate about economic impacts. Correctly analyzing such long-term effects of policy often requires specialized knowledge, such as an understanding of tariffs, trade laws, and supply chains—knowledge that unlettered men don’t have the time or means to learn in depth. Thus, non-elites are criticized for being susceptible to heuristics, mental shortcuts such as endorsements or candidate personality, when voting. [3] Moreover, politicians and media frequently utilize emotional rhetoric, often overemphasizing nationalism, religion, and xenophobia, to appeal to the emotional side of voters. Slogans such as “Take back control” or claims about the UK sending £350 million a week to other nations in the EU are prominent examples of such politicized heuristics. [4] These misconceptions arguably reflect the often-unintended political ignorance of the unlettered and how they can shape policy outcomes. One study found that voters guessed immigrants made up about 20% of the UK’s population, when in reality the figure was closer to 5%. [5] These shortcuts and gaps in knowledge highlight a basic weakness in democratic systems: common voters can vote based more on feelings than facts, and the outcome can steer policy in suboptimal directions. With these problems in mind, the question at hand is, what value does broader political participation have? Should we even bother with the democratic vote when voters are prone to such uninformed decisions? In this essay, I explore whether democracy's inclusive nature is a strength or weakness and whether an elite-driven technocracy offers a more just alternative. Ultimately, I will argue the existence of heuristics and misinformation does not mean the votes of the unlettered are dispensable—common voters share important insights and values that elites are prone to overlook.

Problems of Elite-Only Decision-Making

Policy making rarely presents clear-cut binaries of “good” or “bad.” Perhaps if we had a “utility calculating machine” that could weigh each policy’s total utility, we could objectively assess good and bad policies. But since we don’t, policymaking becomes more about balancing complex, unquantifiable trade-offs between competing subjective values and interests. Environmental policies are one of the most prominent examples: Policies that benefit the environment inherently have to trade off some aspects of industrialization. But how does one objectively prove that one is more important than the other? Sure, some argue that cutting emissions should come above all else, no matter the short-term economic loss. But others disagree, saying economic conditions and living costs also matter, as exemplified in France’s 2018 Yellow Vest Protests. [6] Likewise, Brexit may have been bad for the holistic economy, but can we logically conclude that subjective values such as the desire for more sovereignty or the unwillingness to open borders are less important? Farmers, during Brexit, voiced concerns about economic dislocation and inequality regarding policies such as the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), which forced the UK to accept agricultural imports, undercutting domestic farmers, and fueled fears of losing control over agricultural policy and, more broadly, national sovereignty. Can we conclude that these values are outweighed by macroeconomic progress, based on specialized information? [7]

The interests and values in society are diverse, and it is impossible to logically figure out a single best policy that best meets the interests of all the people. In all policies, some groups will lose out. Inevitably, elites are also prone to information asymmetry. Just as non-elites often fail to assess the long-term implications of policies, elites cannot fully comprehend the lived context of the masses, leading to policies that fail to align with the real needs of the people. Technocratic policy-making often devalues the lived experiences, local knowledge, and value system of communities. For example, when a cultural site has the potential for industrialization, elites will often choose to prioritize economic development. On paper, these cultural sites are an economic inefficiency; however, in reality, demolishing these sites in favor of a mall can lead to deep grievances and, in fact, a decrease in utility. Elite’s failure to capture the deeper utility or value that such spaces hold for communities is detrimental. A contemporary example is the failure of Kwame Nkrumah’s modernization program in Ghana. For example, the Akosombo Dam—Nkrumah’s flagship project—displaced about 80,000 rural residents, breaking up families and displacing them from their ancestral land. Farmers were pushed into collective farms and industrial collectives. [8] State-run agricultural and industrial collective schemes often ignored traditional agricultural knowledge and local climate conditions. [9] New factories, meanwhile, frequently ran below capacity due to poor planning, a mismatch with local skills, and weak domestic demand. [10] These issues, combined with piling foreign debt, led to immense frustration, protests, and the eventual removal of Nkrumah in 1966. [11] A lack of democratic vote takes away the ability of mass voters to signal their preferences without drastic measures like protest. This leaves much room for informational asymmetry of the elite to go unchecked, since they lack the incentive to proactively understand these local contexts. Without broad democratic participation, policymaking becomes more of a deductive conceptual game for elites, which often does not accurately reflect the utility of those directly affected.

Finally, many of the arguments above rest on the assumption that elites or "lettered men" are genuinely committed to promoting the collective good. In reality, however, elites are humans who are often driven by self-interest. Noam Chomsky argued that technocratic governance often masks the interests of corporate and political elites under the guise of "expertise." Democratic plurality, hence, is a means not only to compensate for potential gaps in elite knowledge but also to guard against their intentional misuse of power. [12] Indeed, throughout history, there have been numerous cases where “lettered” elites constructed policies diametrically opposed to the interests of the masses. The Corn Act, a series of laws that enacted tariffs and trade restrictions on imported grain in Britain from 1815 to 1846, was explicitly designed to protect large landowners; the lower and middle classes had to face extraordinarily inflated food prices. [13] Even in modern day, the power of the elites often goes unchecked; thus, there is no guarantee that they will work for the general good. Large corporations often lobby for tax breaks or deregulations at the expense of weakened public services or environmental harm. The recent Supreme Court case Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission allows corporations unlimited expenditure in electoral politics, highlighting how elites, if left unchecked, often use institutions to prioritize self-gain. [14]

Value of Democratic Participation

Elite overreach—whether intentional or due to informational asymmetry—demonstrates the necessity of a democratic system. The votes of the ordinary citizens help check these problems by creating an alignment of interests. In democratic theory, scholars have long debated epistemic democracy, which values democracy for its capacity to produce optimal policy outcomes, and procedural democracy, which focuses on the principle moral value of broad civic participation. While epistemic models focus on the role of informed deliberation and collective intelligence in reaching just outcomes, procedural models underscore democracy’s principal value as a mechanism of fairness and inclusion. [15]

However, both frameworks converge in recognizing the danger of elite domination. Under the epistemic framework, a democratic vote allows society to aggregate diverse preferences. In a referendum, the votes of citizens are a binding decision, such as Brexit. The more perspectives included, the more comprehensive the preferences are; thus, there is a higher likelihood of reaching a consensus that reflects public interest rather than just narrow elite opinions. Furthermore, universal voting structurally aligns the interests of the elite and the masses. In a democracy, multiple parties compete to wield political power, depending on popular vote. Thus, political parties essentially have a structural incentive to proactively seek and aggregate the interests of the masses, thereby creating the most “public utility” in a free market. [16]

Finally, the existence of a universal vote creates a culture of information sharing, allowing for the constant engagement of non-elites. Public discourse and increased input into policy foster a sense of empowerment and agency among the unlettered men, which aligns with the procedural value of democracy. But more importantly, open discourse pressures elites to invest in broad civic education and information, strengthening the epistemic function. J.S. Mill’s argument in On Liberty, which highlights that the improvement of ideas—and by extension society—can only occur if there is consistent public discourse. [17] However, this process depends on the belief of individuals that their participation has real influence. When individuals are denied a vote or an active role in politics, the incentive to engage in discourse dissipates, weakening also the epistemic function of democracy. In return, a lack of civic participation is likely to increase public mistrust of policies. During the COVID-19 pandemic, highly technocratic governments such as China subjugated citizens with strict lockdowns and vaccine mandates with minimal public input, consequently leading to public distrust and protests undermining the legitimacy of the regime. [18] In contrast, New Zealand operated more democratically and transparently while still enforcing lockdown procedures, and despite strict regulations, its policies were met with far greater public trust. [19] Since policy creation in democracies is collaborative, more people are willing to accept the outcome if they believe that it is decided through public discourse.

Importance of a Middle Ground

The Brexit referendum—while from a purely economic lens may have indeed been suboptimal—had the function of exposing rural citizens’ feelings of discontent and exclusion from the globalized economy. In response, post-referendum, politicians shifted towards promoting policies that addressed regional inequalities and immigration regulations, such as the U.K. Solidarity Fund. In other words, even the Brexit referendum signaled important information about the values of a subset population, which was not captured by economic statistics, allowing elites to update their policy directions to more align with broader public conceptions of utility. [20]

The universal vote constantly forces elites to realign their positions to ensure that policies are created not solely based on the value preferences of a few elites but also cater to the lived experiences of the common citizens. At the same time, functional democracies often have methods to allow more technical oversight of the common vote. Expertise is already a part of important sectors of the government, such as the Ministry of Justice and the Defense. While the broad direction of policies is referred back to common people via voting (since policies are not simply data-based but driven by underlying subjective values), Elite bureaucrats and a board of experts usually oversee the technical and logistical aspects of implementation.

Democracy is not safeguarded by the infallibility of the lettered or unlettered men. However, while elites often provide rational choices grounded in analyses, as Edmund Burke iterated, the wisdom of the unlettered man matters—not because it is always rational or far-sighted, but because the universal vote constantly forces elites to justify their ideas, share information, and listen to the needs of the non-elites. This back-and-forth creates debate and prevents political capture by a narrow interest group. Consequently, this leads to policies that are thoughtful and grounded in people’s everyday lives.
