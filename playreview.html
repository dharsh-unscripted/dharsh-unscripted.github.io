Play Review: Angels in America

Published October 2025

My favorite play is Angels in America by Tony Kushner. It defies the traditional notion of theater being linear, instead blending a sense of political urgency, spiritual inquiry, and painfully relatable arcs into one cohesive piece of written work. At its heart, it is not just a story of individual characters, but a visceral work that forces the audience to witness the rigid society in which people were forced to live and suffer. It smashes the illusion of American exceptionalism. Set during the AIDS crisis and the Reagan era of conservatism, the play takes the audience on a journey with multiple characters as they learn to navigate moral responsibility amidst chaos.

Contrary to most plays, Kushner fragments the narrative across multiple storylines. By doing so, he forces the audience away from a singular moral interpretation and instead compels them to weigh competing perspectives. Each character responds differently to crisis; Joe Pitt’s repression and Roy Cohn’s ideological cruelty are not presented as a unified message but as clashing worldviews. Through these collisions, their limitations are exposed, and the audience is pushed into discomfort and reflection. This sustained engagement transforms the play from a performance into something that deeply resonates with the audience’s own lives.

Moreover, Kushner’s rejection of strict realism allows him to expose interior and cultural trauma. The play incorporates supernatural elements such as hallucinations, ancestral ghosts, and angelic visitations. Prior’s visions make visible the suffering of those living with AIDS—suffering that society often ignored. The Angel, traditionally a symbol of goodness, is instead used to challenge the idea that authority is inherently moral. Rather than guiding humanity, the Angel resists change, mirroring rigid societal structures that prioritize order over compassion. These elements reveal hidden oppression and emphasize that much of society’s rigidity stems from forces larger than the individual.

Through striking characterization, Kushner fuses politics with intimate human experiences. Roy Cohn initially appears as a symbol of conservatism, but his refusal to acknowledge his sexuality reflects a system that rewards conformity. His character becomes a representation of a government that ignored the AIDS crisis. This blending of the political and personal allows the play to remain deeply relevant, exposing how ideology shapes individual lives.

A Defining Scene: Perestroika

My favorite scene occurs in the Perestroika portion of the play, where Prior Walter confronts the Angel. She claims that human progress has destabilized heaven and demands that humanity stop evolving. The Angel represents a conservative desire to preserve order and resist change.

Kushner critiques this demand as a fear of transformation. Just as the Angel prioritizes cosmic stability over human suffering, the Reagan era prioritized order over addressing the AIDS crisis. However, Prior rejects her demand, marking his transformation from a passive sufferer into someone willing to challenge authority. This moment deeply resonated with me, pushing me to reflect on powerlessness and the role individuals play in defending progress.

The scene is further elevated by Kushner’s use of theatrical physicality. The Angel’s violent entrance disrupts expectations of divine serenity, using light and sound to create chaos. This spectacle reframes authority as something intrusive and destabilizing rather than inherently benevolent.

Prior himself is a compelling figure because he is not a traditional hero. He is vulnerable, frightened, and uncertain—yet he evolves into someone capable of resistance. His journey suggests that justice does not require extraordinary power, but rather the courage to confront injustice despite fear. This message feels especially relevant today, where many experience a sense of helplessness in the face of systemic issues.

I often reflect on this scene when I witness injustice. Kushner reminds us that fear and vulnerability are natural, but they should not prevent action. Through powerful dialogue, theatrical innovation, and deeply human characters, this scene conveys a lasting message: progress is not inevitable—it must be defended with courage and conviction.
