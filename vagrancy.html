Social Vagrancy in South Korea

Published November 2025

Throughout Korean history, the definition of vagrancy has been fluid, as the presence of homeless and idle populations has presented a complex social challenge to governments of different periods, each of which has responded to the issue differently due to political, social, and economic forces. Throughout different epochs of history, vagrancy has mirrored the anxieties of the elite class, whether it be on perceived social illness or national modernization. Moreover, during Korea’s period of National Modernization, vagrants were forced to work behind the scenes, contributing immensely to Korea’s infrastructure and export boom. Behind the curtain of the modern nation we now know Korea to be, lies a dark history of exploitation of marginalized individuals perceived as a societal ill. Has the nation effaced such notions, or are modern policies continuing this age-old discrimination?

Post-Korean War Modernization Attempts

The Korean War, which spanned from 1950 to 1953, was fought between North Korea, which was backed by China and Russia, and South Korea, backed by the United States and United Nations forces. While an armistice between the two blocs ended active fighting, the Korean peninsula was divided along the 38th parallel.

In South Korea, more than 100,000 children were orphaned, and millions of individuals were displaced. The state, eager to project stability and progress to other nations, subjugated citizens to strict developmental and social frameworks. Syngman Rhee, supported by the United States due to his anti-communist stance in politics, rose to become South Korea’s first president. His role in Korean society was defined as a consolidation of the developmental state. This model prioritized economic growth above all else, focusing on rapid industrialization, urban restructuring, and state-directed resource mobilization as a means of state-building.

Park Chung-Hee’s Regime and Forced Labor Programs

Park Chung-Hee’s military regime was established in 1961. After the coup, the authorities locked up almost 68,000 vagrants and forced 1,800 to work on undeveloped lands from 1962 to 1966 under the guise of social cleansing.

The regime established a state-run labor program across the nation that forcibly relocated individuals who were classified as vagrants—such as homeless people, orphans, delinquents, and mentally or physically disabled individuals—to rural areas. Individuals who were deemed vagrants by the police and military personnel patrolling the streets were rounded up and were then transported to rural areas, where they were forced to work on various projects. These individuals were subject to labor without any compensation.

While this program was run under the guise of rehabilitation and national service, it was part of a broader strategy to use the vagrant populace as a means of human resources for national development projects, such as land reclamation and infrastructure. Within labor programs, individuals were subject to grueling work conditions, physical abuse, and malnutrition. In one such program, 1770 vagrants were transported to Seosan to work on land reclamation projects. Vagrants within the Seosan reclamation project were kept in army-style barracks and were only given meager meals, consisting of a bowl of rice and thin soup. This forced many individuals to catch and eat frogs, snakes, and rats.

Waste Picker Camps

The social exclusion of vagrants was perpetuated by house waste picker camps. The camp was titled the Work Reconstruction Camp (WRC, 켈로재건대). Camps nationwide had 7,357 inmates, and almost half (3,093) were located within Seoul.

Police officers could pick up a vagrant and force them into these camps. Waste pickers scavenged the streets, collecting large quantities of waste daily. Despite their labor, the camps did not provide meaningful economic mobility, instead reinforcing exploitation through intermediaries and authorities.

Living conditions were harsh. Waste pickers were housed in overcrowded bunkhouses or makeshift shanties. They were forced to wear identification tags and adhere to strict behavioral codes, reinforcing their separation from mainstream society. These camps prioritized control and suppression of visible poverty rather than genuine rehabilitation.

The Brothers Home

In the mid-1970s, the Ministry of Health and Social Affairs introduced the Social Welfare Work Act (사회복지사업법). Soon after, the Ministry of Home Affairs enacted the Vagrancy Directive, Ordinance 410 (내무부 훈령 제410호), allowing authorities to detain individuals deemed “vagrants.”

The Brothers Home, originally an orphanage, became one of the most infamous institutions under this system. It was reclassified as a social welfare corporation, allowing it to receive state funding per detainee. This created incentives to detain as many individuals as possible.

Those detained ranged widely—from orphans and the disabled to random civilians perceived as “socially suspect.” Many were forcibly taken by authorities. Inside, detainees were subjected to forced labor in farms and factories producing goods for export, including textiles and manufactured products.

The institution expanded into multiple factories, relying entirely on unpaid labor from detainees. Numerous accounts describe abuse, exploitation, and severe human rights violations.

Today, South Korea is celebrated for its rapid development. However, the silenced voices of those subjected to these systems remain a powerful reminder of the inequalities embedded within that progress. The treatment of vagrants reveals how states have historically justified exclusion and exploitation in the name of modernization and order.
