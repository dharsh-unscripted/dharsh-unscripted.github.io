The Green Revolution in India: Whither Nutrition?

Published August 3, 2025

Kari, a 45-year-old woman in Bihar, is paid in kind in wheat and rice for the harvesting work she does at local fields. With her small income, she feeds her family. However, despite years of bumper harvests, Kari and millions like her across India face a paradoxical struggle: malnutrition.

This paradox is rooted in India’s history of agricultural transformation. After India’s independence in 1947, India’s agricultural productivity lagged far behind its rapidly growing population. In the face of a looming food crisis, the Indian Green Revolution modernized Indian agriculture: indigenous seeds were replaced with high-yielding varieties (HYV), and irrigation technologies and fertilizers were introduced nationally. As a consequence of these efforts, agricultural productivity soared, and India became the world’s largest producer of rice and second largest producer of wheat.

The Green Revolution in India is rightly praised for its success in ensuring food security, since it was India’s escape from widespread Malthusian speculations of the 1950s and 60s that India would run into ‘natural’ population limits and thus mass starvation. However, the model of monocultures, subsidies, and market dynamics that made the Green Revolution such a large success has also catalyzed persistent poverty and a “hidden hunger” crisis.

The growth of monocultures and subsidies has been particularly detrimental for poverty in rural India. Starting in the Green Revolution, a glut of subsidies was given for urea, a fertilizer that massively increased rice and wheat yield. However, monoculture farming practices and overuse of fertilizers have depleted millions of hectares of land. Consequently, although the total quantity of crop harvested is higher than before the Green Revolution, the increase in production quantity has undershot predictions. The average market value of crops has eroded far more than the increase in yield, meaning a higher yield has paradoxically punished impoverished rural families.

Larger farms avoid soil erosion by leaving parts of their land fallow, as well as price decreases by navigating the Indian federal government’s byzantine system of subsidies. However, small-scale and low-income farmers can neither afford to leave land fallow nor access government subsidies to protect against the vicissitudes of markets. As a result, most small farmers – over 50 percent of the rural Indian population – are trapped in a cycle of debt. In Punjab, the most agriculturally productive state in India, 89 percent of farmers are in debt due to rising costs associated with water and fertilizer. The combination of these factors has left more than 40 percent of Indian farmers below the poverty line.

In addition to entrenching rural poverty, the Green Revolution also created dynamics that are central to India’s public health crisis of ‘hidden hunger,’ or micronutrient deficiencies that persist even with adequate calorie intake. India suffers from widespread deficiencies in iron, iodine, zinc, vitamins A and D – all nutrients relatively lacking in energy-dense staples like rice and wheat, which have overwhelmingly replaced micronutrient-rich crops in the average Indian diet.

This change in diet in large part owes to the Green Revolution’s focus on increasing the yield of HYV staples through subsidies. The Minimum Support Price (MSP), introduced to protect farmers against price fluctuations, resulted in higher price realization for rice and wheat and skewed cultivation in favor of these HYV crops. But this skew also significantly decreased cultivation of micronutrient-rich crops, especially in large-scale agricultural operations. According to the 2020 National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) survey, 24 percent of paddy and 21 percent of wheat output was procured by the government under the MSP scheme. However, the equivalent figure for coarse cereals with millets and pulses were respectively less than 5 percent. Accordingly, the share of gross cropped area for coarse cereals such as millets has declined from 30 percent to 11 percent, and the area under pulses has remained stagnant at around 14 percent from the years 1950 to 2020, despite a four-fold increase in the Indian population. Decreased coarse grains and pulses cultivation has predictably resulted in their decreased availability. The supply of pulses, for instance, has decreased from 25.2 kg/capita to 17.5 kg/capita during 1961-2020.

The outcome of this unavailability has been the pricing out of the average Indian family from nutritious diets. According to a 2023 report on food security and nutrition by five UN agencies, 74.1 percent of Indians are unable to afford a nutritious diet: the monthly costs for a nutritious diet come to around 10,800 rupees (US$129) for a four-person household, but monthly disposable incomes for equivalent households are stagnant at 10,000 rupees (US $119).

The MSP, indeed, is a double-subsidy scheme in that it distributes the surplus food bought up through MSP to disadvantaged families at subsidized prices through the Public Distribution System (PDS), which 67 percent of the Indian population depends on. These consumption subsidies reinforce the relative affordability and therefore attractiveness of micronutrient-deficient HYVs over indigenous crops. The relative lack of MSP supply of millets and pulses, then, has created conditions ripe for the prevalence of micronutrient-deficient diets among disadvantaged Indian families.

Micronutrient deficiencies cause frequent infections as well as high rates of anemia. In India, anemia caused by iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiencies affects a shocking 68 percent of children aged 6–59 months. Women and young girls are especially susceptible to micronutrient deficiencies, as their bodies require larger amounts of specific micronutrients, such as iron, to balance the loss of micronutrients during menstruation and lactation. Despite this, the unspoken rule in most traditional households is to leave the largest proportion of food to the men and boys, leaving women and girls even more at risk of micronutrient deficiencies and health complications. During pregnancy, deficiencies in micronutrients are thus passed down to children from their mothers, continuing the cycle of hidden hunger.

The longer-term socioeconomic impacts of this malnutrition are profound, particularly due to the lifetime cognitive and emotional deficits that it causes. For millions of rural families in India, education is often the only ticket for social mobility. And so children’s performance in school directly affects their chances of breaking cycles of poverty. Since stunting directly correlates with lower performance in school, malnutrition helps trap impoverished households in intergenerational poverty.

Beyond problems of social mobility, micronutrient deficiencies can lead to impairment in brain development, consequently leading to a host of other social problems. Cognitive impairment leads to lower general work productivity, consequently lower lifetime earnings prospects at every stage of life. Furthermore, impairment may also impact emotional regulation, which is linked to increased rates of crime, violence, and abuse. The relatively high rates of violent crime in rural areas may be, in this context, understood as partially linked to levels of malnutrition.

As the world looks toward more green revolutions to support food supply in the face of climate change, the Indian experience serves as a valuable yet cautionary tale. On the one hand, the relentless focus on increasing yield helped the nation avoid famine. On the other hand, it has deepened poverty and led to a whole host of health and social problems. Future green revolutionaries should take heed: beyond yield, promoting high-nutrient crops at affordable prices and preventing soil depletion are essential to create a more nutritious and equitable future for all.
